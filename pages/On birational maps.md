- Here's my understanding of the definition of birational maps: inclusions of (dense) open subsets are birational, and isomorphisms are obviously birational. All birational maps are generated by these, as long as we allow the first class to be inverted.
- Here's why:
- **Claim.** Let $X,Y$ be integral schemes locally of finite type over some base $S$. Let $\phi: K(Y) \to K(X)$ be an isomorphism. Then there are non-empty open subsets $U \subset X$ and $V \subset Y$ such that $U \xrightarrow{\;\simeq\;} V$.
	- This loc finite type condition is crucial. Localizations, e.g. $k[t] \to k(t)$ or $\Z \to \Z_{(p)}$ are clearly counterexamples without it.
- ## Proof
- ### Step 1, affine case
- Suppose $X=\Spec B$, $Y=\Spec A$. Further suppose there is a morphism $f:X \to Y$ inducing $\phi$.
	- Then we can write $\phi:A \hookrightarrow B$, which is injective and of finite type.
- Let $B=A[b_1,\cdots,b_n]$. Then $b_i \in \Frac B = \Frac A$, so we can write $b_i=a_i/s_i$ where $a_i,s_i\in A$.
- Put $s=s_1\cdots s_n$. Clearly, $A_s \simeq B_s$ via $\phi$.
- ### Step 2
- In general, pick open subsets $\Spec B \subset X$ and $\Spec A \subset Y$, both over some $\Spec R\subset S$.
- Let $A=R[a_1,\cdots,a_n]$ and find a common demoninator $t$ in $B$ of the $\phi(a_i)$. This gives a ring homomorphism
  $$A \longto B_t$$
- Now we can apply Step 1, giving us an isomorphism between some distinguished opens
  $$A_s \xrightarrow{\;\simeq\;} B_{t \cdot \phi(s)}.$$
- **QED**
- ---
- This identifies all birational maps (which are not strictly morphisms). What about birational morphisms?
- **Definition.** Let $X, Y$ be integral and locally of finite type over a scheme $S$. We say $f:X \to Y$ is a birational morphism, if it is dominant and induces an isomorphism $\phi: K(Y) \to K(X)$.
- **Claim** These form the class of weak equivalences generated by dense open inclusions, in the sense that it is the smallest class of morphisms containing all isomorphisms and dense open inclusions that satisfy the [2-out-of-3](https://ncatlab.org/nlab/show/category+with+weak+equivalences) condition.
	- Proof. By Step 1 above, we know all birational morphisms are contained this way, by
	  $$X \longleftarrow U \simeq V \longto Y$$
	  and 2-out-of-3.
	- It remains to show that birational morphisms satisfy 2-out-of-3. Field isomorphisms clearly satisfy this, so we only need to treat dominance. Since our schemes are irreducible, dominance is equivalent to $f(\eta_X)=\eta_Y$.
	- Consider $f:X\to Y$ and $g:Y\to Z$. The only nontrivial case is when $gf$ and $g$ are birational morphisms, so
	  $$g\circ f(\eta_X) = \eta_Z\quad \text{and}\;\;\;\; g(\eta_Y)=\eta_Z.$$
	- Since $g$ is birational, the composition
	  $$K(Z) = \mc{O}$$
